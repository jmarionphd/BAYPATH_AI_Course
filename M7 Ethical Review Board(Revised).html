What is a Human Ethics Review Board?

A Human Ethics Review Board—often called an Institutional Review Board (IRB) in the US or Research Ethics Board (REB) in Canada—is a committee formally designated to approve, monitor, and review biomedical and behavioral research involving humans.

Its primary mandate is to protect the rights and welfare of human research subjects, ensuring that research is conducted ethically and responsibly.

Why Are They Essential?

ERBs were established in response to past research abuses where participants were harmed. They serve several critical functions in the research ecosystem.

Protect Participants

Safeguards individuals from physical or psychological harm, upholding their dignity and autonomy.

Ensure Compliance

Guarantees that research adheres to federal regulations, state laws, and institutional policies.

Build Public Trust

Maintains public confidence in the integrity of the research enterprise, which is vital for participation and funding.

The Three Core Ethical Principles

The work of all ERBs is guided by the principles outlined in the Belmont Report (1979), a foundational document in research ethics.

1. Respect for Persons

Treat individuals as autonomous agents. Protect those with diminished autonomy. This is the basis for Informed Consent.

2. Beneficence

"Do no harm." Maximize possible benefits and minimize possible harms. This is the basis for the Risk/Benefit Assessment.

3. Justice

Ensure a fair distribution of the costs and benefits of research. Avoid exploiting vulnerable populations. This is the basis for Fair Subject Selection.

Key Responsibilities for Researchers

A successful and ethical study begins with a thorough and thoughtful submission to the ERB.

Researchers must clearly articulate:

  • Research Protocol: What are the research questions? What procedures will be used?
  • Risk Assessment: What are the potential risks (physical, psychological, social) and how will they be minimized?
  • Informed Consent Process: How will participants be informed about the study? The consent form must be clear, voluntary, and comprehensive.
  • Data Protection: How will participant privacy and data confidentiality be maintained and secured?
  • Subject Selection: Who will be recruited and is the selection equitable? Are there protections for vulnerable groups (e.g., children, prisoners)?

Levels of ERB Review

Not all research requires the same level of scrutiny. Studies are categorized based on their level of risk to participants.

Exempt

Poses no more than minimal risk. Examples: anonymous surveys, analysis of existing anonymous data, observing public behavior.

Expedited

Poses no more than minimal risk but involves activities that fall into specific regulated categories. Reviewed by one or two ERB members. Example: collection of blood samples by finger stick.

Full Board

Poses more than minimal risk or involves vulnerable populations. Requires review by the entire convened ERB committee at a meeting.

Example Case: A Social Media Research Study

Let's consider the ethics of a study analyzing public social media posts.

The Proposal: A researcher wants to analyze 1 million public Twitter posts that mention a specific antidepressant to study self-reported side effects.

Key Ethical Questions for the ERB:

  • Is "public" data truly public? Users may not reasonably expect their posts to be used in formal research.
  • What is the risk of re-identification? Could quoting a "anonymized" post lead back to the original user's profile?
  • Is there a risk of harm? The topic is sensitive. Could the study's findings stigmatize a group of individuals?

Likely ERB Decision: May qualify for Expedited Review. The board would likely require the researcher to implement strict data security measures, aggregate all findings so no individual can be identified, and refrain from quoting any posts directly in publications.

Key Takeaways

→

Protection Over Progress. The primary goal of an ERB is to protect human participants; this objective always comes before the goals of the research itself.

→

ERBs are Partners, Not Obstacles. They are a crucial resource for helping researchers design better, safer, and more ethical studies.

→

Ethics is the Foundation of Good Science. Research that is not conducted ethically lacks scientific and social validity.

→

The Process Starts Before the Research. Engaging with the ERB is one of the first steps in the research process, not the last.
